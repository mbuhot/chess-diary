### Game Overview

This was an exciting one. Black came out of the opening strongly, developing quickly and pointing a lot of material at my king, and for a while I felt like I was just responding to their moves. But I got a bit of breathing room by trading off their light-squared bishop, remembered to counterattack in the centre rather than defend, and once Black castled queenside the game turned into a race to the opposite wing. From there I landed a knight on e5, sacrificed a bishop to drag the king into the open, hunted it down with the queen, and eventually promoted. A few more forcing blows in the endgame and Black resigned into a promotion mate.

### The Opening (Moves 1-5)

**1. d4 d5 2. Nf3 Nc6 3. e3 Bf5**

Black played one of the more annoying replies to my d4. Putting the bishop on **3...Bf5** takes away the natural **Bd3**, so my light-squared bishop loses its best square.

**6. b3 Ne4 7. O-O Bd6 8. Bb2 Qf6 9. Nbd2 Qg6**

By move nine Black had done well to develop the pieces. They'd delayed castling and it was safe for them to castle either side. They had a lot of material pointed in the general direction of my castled king, and I felt like I was responding to Black's moves rather than controlling the flow of the game.

### Trading Off and Turning to the Centre (Moves 10-13)

**10. Nh4 Qh6 11. Ndf3 Bg4 12. h3 Bxf3 13. Nxf3 O-O-O**

I got a bit of room to breathe by trading Black's light-squared bishop for my knight. Then I remembered the principle that when the opponent is attacking on the flank, you need to counterattack in the centre. So rather than trying to concentrate all my pieces over on the kingside, I started looking for a central break.

Black chose to castle queenside, and I thought this gave me an opportunity. It reinforced the plan to counterattack the opposite side to my own king, as fast as possible, to take the pressure off Black's attack.

### The Bishop Sacrifice (Moves 19-22)

**19. Bxa6+ Kd7 20. b5 cxb5 21. Bxb5+ c6**

This was my chance. I could see **19. Bxa6+**, and I also had a diagonal available to bring the queen over to a4 and hit the undefended pawn on c6. Black tucked the king in on d7, and I pressed the attack with **20. b5**, inviting Black to take the pawn so that I could recapture with check.

### The Exchange Sacrifice (Moves 29-32)

**29...Nc4 30. Rac1 Bd6**

Black did their best to free up the king with **29...Nc4** and **30...Bd6**, but I had a nice tactic.

**31. Rxc4 dxc4 32. Qxd6+ Ke8**

### Forcing the Queen Trade (Moves 33-34)

**33. Qe5 Qxe5 34. dxe5**

After some analysis I realised that Black could force a queen trade with something like **Qe7**. Given that I was up a piece with a passed pawn over on the a-file, I decided to force the queen trade myself with **33. Qe5**, skewering the queen and rook on the diagonal, so Black was obliged to take. I could see a fairly straightforward path to victory even if it was a little imprecise. On reflection, **Rc1** to target the pawn on c4 would have been a good move here.

### Converting the Advantage (Moves 35-40)

**35. a4 Rb8 36. Bc3 Rb3 37. Rc1 Ra3 38. a5 Kd7 39. Bb4 Rb3 40. Rxc4**

After the exchange, Black brought the rook over to the b-file to attack my undefended bishop. This was a little disappointing, because I'd calculated this line and I should have played **Bd4**, giving the bishop a nice outpost, and then **Ra1** to defend the pawn. Instead I played the still good but not quite as powerful **36. Bc3**, which blocks in Black's pawn but also gives Black enough time to bring the rook in and get behind my pawn.

I then found **39. Bb4** to target Black's rook, and as Black moved the rook away I grabbed the pawn on c4 with **40. Rxc4**. From here I was able to manoeuvre my pieces up towards Black's king while Black's rook was tied up targeting my pawn, trying to prevent a promotion.

### The Finish (Moves 41-46)

**41. Bd6 Rb1+ 42. Kh2 Ra1 43. Rc5 f6 44. Rc7+ Kd8 45. exf6 Ke8 46. f7+**

Black pushed **43...f6**, targeting the pawn that was defending my bishop, but it was a mistake. I checked with the rook, **44. Rc7+**, abandoning the defence of the a5 pawn, and then took on f6 with **45. exf6**, threatening to promote the f-pawn.

Black made one final king move, **45...Ke8**, trying to prevent the promotion, but I could push **46. f7+**. The only move would be **Kd8**, as the f8 square was guarded by my bishop, leading to a beautiful promotion checkmate with **f8=Q#**. Black resigned.

### Engine Review

Not a bad game from me on the numbers: 80.5% accuracy against 73.4% for my opponent, with a game rating of 1550 versus 1000. My opponent had two blunders and four mistakes, against just two mistakes for me, plus one brilliant move on my side.

The engine confirmed that my discomfort in the opening was justified. It flagged **11. Ndf3** as a mistake: Black could sacrifice the dark-squared bishop with **11...Bxh2+**, and after I recapture with the knight, **12...Qxh4** wins the material straight back, leaving Black a pawn up with my king weakened. Neither of my knight moves, **10. Nh4** or **11. Ndf3**, was the way to go. It would have been best to play **10. c4** and start cracking open the centre before I got drawn any further into Black's attack.

Black's first blunder was **17...Qf6**. They needed to capture my knight with **17...Nxe5**. My follow-up wasn't completely accurate either: instead of **18. Nxc6**, the engine liked **18. Qa4**, swinging the queen across to prepare the attack. Black's second blunder was **20...cxb5**, which simply invited my bishop to recapture with check.

The brilliant move was **22. Bxc6+**, sacrificing the bishop to open up the king. During the king hunt, **26. c6+** was a slight misstep: it let the king escape towards e8, whereas **26. Qa4+** would have kept it penned in.

In the endgame the engine likes my exchange sacrifice **31. Rxc4** as the best move in the position, which is nice to see. It was less keen on **33. Qe5**. Rather than trade queens straight away, it preferred to keep them on with **33. Bc3**, delaying the trade for a few moves and steering into an endgame where I end up a piece or two ahead once the queens finally come off. That fits my own feeling afterwards that **Rc1**, hitting the c4 pawn, was cleaner than forcing the trade. It also agreed that **36. Bd4** was a stronger square than the **Bc3** I played.

### Reflections

**What went well:**

- Surviving Black's initial attack and then coming up with a plan of my own. Trading off the light-squared bishop for some breathing room, then counterattacking in the centre with **c4** rather than trying to defend the kingside.
- The piece sacrifice and the attack that followed. Seeing the opportunity to sacrifice a bishop with **22. Bxc6+** to really open up Black's king, and then pressing the attack with the queen and the passed c-pawn. That is the part that won me the game.
- The finish. Seeing that **46. f7+** forced the king to d8 and led to a promotion mate on f8.

**What to work on:**

- The opening. Those initial knight moves, **10. Nh4** and **11. Ndf3**, just played into Black's hand rather than improving my own position. I was uncomfortable and on the back foot, and I was fortunate Black didn't press the advantage.
- Knowing when to push a pawn in front of my king. Here a pawn move to blunt Black's pieces on the diagonals would have been preferable, even though putting a pawn in front of the king is weakening in itself.
- Precision in winning positions. **33. Qe5** was a little imprecise when keeping the queens on was cleaner, and **36. Bc3** instead of **Bd4** gave Black's rook time to get active.
