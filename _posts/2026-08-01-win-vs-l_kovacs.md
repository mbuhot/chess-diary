### Game Overview

This was a fairly straightforward one. I turned a small early edge into being up a pawn, then two, then finally three, and by the end my opponent had little chance of stopping me from promoting. It was strong positional play without much tactical excitement in the game itself, and it finished with a resignation. The one bit of fireworks was hiding in the engine lines, not on the board, and I have saved that for the end.

---

### The Anti-Fianchetto Setup (Moves 1-6)

**1. d4 Nf6 2. Nf3 g6 3. Nc3 d5 4. Bf4 Bg7 5. Qd2 O-O 6. Bh6**

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/images/2026-08-01-game2/move11.png' | relative_url }}" alt="After 6. Bh6">
  <figcaption>After 6. Bh6: the bishop confronts the fianchettoed bishop on g7, backed by the queen on d2.</figcaption>
</figure>

I started with my usual d4 and Nf3 setup, then noticed my opponent was going for some kind of King's Indian style arrangement with the kingside fianchetto. I remembered there is a very annoying way of playing against that: bringing the bishop out to **Bh6** supported by a queen on **Qd2**. It is a setup I personally find frustrating when I play the King's Indian from the black side, so I thought this was my chance to play it from the white side.

---

### Trading the Fianchetto Bishop (Moves 6-10)

**6...Bg4 7. h4 Ne4 8. Nxe4 dxe4 9. Ne5 Bf5 10. Bxg7 Kxg7**

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/images/2026-08-01-game2/move20.png' | relative_url }}" alt="After 10...Kxg7">
  <figcaption>After 10...Kxg7: the dark-squared bishop is gone and the knight sits on e5.</figcaption>
</figure>

By move 10 I had succeeded in eliminating the dark-squared bishop on g7, which left Black's king surrounded by weak dark squares. I had also pushed the h-pawn up to **h4**, which gives me a lever to try to prise open the h-file later on. My knight landed firmly on **e5**, right in the heart of Black's position.

---

### The Peak (Moves 11-13)

**11. h5 Rh8 12. g4 e3 13. Qxe3**

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/images/2026-08-01-game2/move25.png' | relative_url }}" alt="After 13. Qxe3">
  <figcaption>After 13. Qxe3: knight planted on e5, pawns rolling on g4 and h5, but my king is still in the centre.</figcaption>
</figure>

By move 13 things were looking pretty good. The only thing I did not love about the position was that my king was still in the centre and Black still had a queen on the board. Everything else was in my favour: better development, the knight firmly planted on **e5**, and pawns on **g4** and **h5** ready to storm forward. As it turned out I did not convert this directly into a quick win. I had to grind it out into a rook and pawn endgame, but I was happy to have this kind of position out of the opening.

---

### Trades and the Queen Swap (Moves 13-19)

**13...Bxc2 14. hxg6 Bxg6 15. Qh6+ Kg8 16. Nxg6 fxg6 17. O-O-O**

Black grabbed the pawn on **c2** while I turned to the kingside. **14. hxg6** opened the h-file, I checked with **15. Qh6+**, and after **16. Nxg6 fxg6** I finally castled long with **17. O-O-O**, tucking my king safely away on the queenside.

**17...Qf8 18. e3 Qxh6 19. Rxh6**

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/images/2026-08-01-game2/move37.png' | relative_url }}" alt="After 19. Rxh6">

</figure>

Then I played **18. e3**, which invited Black to trade queens on h6. Black took the offer with **18...Qxh6**, and I recaptured with the rook on **19. Rxh6**. With the queens gone, the character of the game changed and it became a matter of technique.

---

### Breaking the Centre (Moves 19-23)

**19...Nc6 20. Bc4+ Kg7 21. g5 e5 22. Be6 exd4 23. exd4**

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/images/2026-08-01-game2/move45.png' | relative_url }}" alt="After 23. exd4">
  <figcaption>After 23. exd4: the central pawn chain has been traded down.</figcaption>

I developed the bishop to **c4** with check and pushed **21. g5** to gain more space. Black struck back in the centre with **21...e5**, and after **22. Be6** I allowed **22...exd4**, which broke up the nice pawn chain I had running from f2 to d4. I recaptured with **23. exd4** to keep a pawn on that square.

### Infiltrating on the Queenside (Moves 23-29)

**23...Rad8 24. d5 Ne5 25. f4 Nf7 26. Rh3 c6 27. Rhd3 cxd5 28. Bxd5 h5 29. Bxb7**

<figure>

I pushed **24. d5** and Black rerouted the knight from **e5** back to **f7**. Then Black played **26...c6** to attack the d5 pawn, but d5 was protected twice, once by the bishop and once by the rook, so hitting it just led to trades. After **27. Rhd3 cxd5 28. Bxd5**, Black pushed **28...h5** and I swung the bishop across to take on **b7** with **29. Bxb7**, coming out a pawn up with my pieces the more active.

### The Rook Endgame (Moves 29-34)

**29...Rxd3 30. Rxd3 h4 31. Rh3 Nd6 32. Ba6 Kf7 33. Bd3 Nf5 34. Bxf5 gxf5**

The rooks came off with **29...Rxd3 30. Rxd3**, and Black pushed the passed h-pawn with **30...h4**. I stopped it with **31. Rh3**, then manoeuvred the bishop over with **32. Ba6** and **33. Bd3** before taking the knight with **34. Bxf5**. Black recaptured **34...gxf5**, and we reached a clean rook endgame where the f5 pawn was weak.

### The King March (Moves 35-36)

**35. Kd2 Ke6 36. Kd3 Kd5**

I started to activate my king with **35. Kd2** and **36. Kd3**. Black brought his own king forward to **d5**, I assume to try to block the advance of my a and b pawns. But it was a mistake, because the f5 pawn became weak the moment Black's king abandoned it.

I pushed **37. b4** and **39. a4**, and as Black's king came across towards the queenside to try to defend those pawns, my king went the other way. **40. Ke5** and **41. Kxf5** picked up the weak pawn, and now I was two pawns to the good with a clear path forward.

### The Finish (Moves 41-46)

**41...Kd5 42. g6 Rf8+ 43. Kg5 Ke6 44. Rxh4 Rf5+ 45. Kh6 Rf6 46. Kh7**

I pushed the g-pawn to **42. g6**, and after **44. Rxh4** the last kingside pawn fell and I was three pawns up. From there the plan was simple: walk my king up with **45. Kh6** and **46. Kh7** to shepherd the passed g-pawn all the way to promotion. With no way to stop a new queen, Black resigned.

### Engine Review

The engine liked my position from early on. Even with material level after the opening, it had me around +2.5 once the dark-squared bishop was gone, and by move 13 it was up to +4.5. That is where I should have been looking for a knockout, because the advantage did not survive the middlegame cleanly.

My first real slip was **18. e3**, which handed Black the queen trade on h6 and dropped the evaluation back to about +1. Then **22. Be6** was a mistake too, letting Black take on d4 and bringing the position back to essentially level. The engine also flagged Black's **26...c6**: since d5 was defended twice, hitting it did not achieve much. The engine's preferred plan for me there was to swing the rook back to the b-file to attack b7 straight away. In that line White wins the b7 pawn, infiltrates with a rook on the seventh rank, and the evaluation jumps to around +4.7, with a threat to win the knight with check supported by the bishop on e6.

In the endgame it did not love **31. Rh3**. Rather than tie my rook down to blocking the h-pawn, it preferred **31. Bg2**, keeping the bishop active on the long diagonal where it already covered the pawn's path. Then **34. Bxf5** was a mistake that, with best play, the engine says Black could have held to a draw. To keep the win in hand I should have activated my king first with **34. Kd2** and started pushing the a and b pawns, leaving Black's pieces tied to the kingside. Once Black's king wandered off to the queenside and I picked up f5, the evaluation climbed back to +5.9 and the win was secure.

The most interesting part is what the engine found back at move 13. From that peak, best play leads to a forced checkmate by around move 30. Instead of recapturing the bishop on c2, White simply storms the kingside, and the whole thing is built around a brilliant knight sacrifice on f7. The king even walks to f1 rather than castling, because the attack down the open h-file is that strong. Beautiful stuff to look at, even if I never got the chance to play it.

### Reflections

**What went well:**

- Recognising the King's Indian style setup and getting to play the Bh6 and Qd2 plan from the white side. Trading off the fianchettoed bishop left Black's king on a field of weak dark squares.
- The king march in the endgame. Sending my king across to the kingside while Black's king was tied to defending the a and b pawns, then swinging back to win the f5 pawn, was the idea that opened the floodgates to a three-pawn advantage.
- Grinding the technical win. This one was never flashy, but I converted a rook endgame cleanly enough once I got the extra pawns rolling.

**What to work on:**

- Converting a big advantage more directly. I was winning at move 13 and then let it slip back towards level with **18. e3** and **22. Be6**. A cleaner finish would have saved a lot of grinding.
- Not tying my own pieces down. **31. Rh3** blocked the pawn but left the rook passive, when **Bg2** kept it active on the diagonal.
- Endgame precision. **34. Bxf5** could have thrown away the win entirely. Activating the king first and pushing the queenside pawns was the safer route to keeping the advantage.
