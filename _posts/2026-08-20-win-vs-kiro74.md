### Game Overview

Chess.com calls this one the King's Pawn Opening: King's Knight, Gunderam Gambit. Not one I'm familiar with. I guess it's characterised by the c6 push, which allows the queen to come out to b6 or a5.

I decided to play it like a Scotch Game, push d4 defended by the knight on f3. There was a brief moment of fear when Black played the bishop to h3 with their queen on g6, threatening checkmate in one. I traded queens, took a knight to e6, and won the d-pawn. Then it crumbled: a knight fork cost me a rook, and I made a losing bishop move on move 30. Black flagged before they could punish it.

**30.Bd4**

I moved my bishop to d4, hoping to fork the rooks when I can take on f6. However, the engine shows that after **30.Bd4** Black can play **30...Kc7**, attacking my rook and leaving it no retreat down the d-file, because my own bishop is now standing on d4. The rook has to shuffle along the sixth rank, and after **31.Bxf6 Kxd6** I'm down material and likely losing the game.

Luckily for me, my opponent was a careful thinker and had used up a lot of time considering their moves. They lost on time when I'd put my bishop on d4.

### Engine Review

The main thing I missed was the idea to sacrifice the rook for the knight, because it's a very interesting line. **25.Rxf3 gxf3 26.Rxd7 Rxd7 27.Bxd7**. Stockfish rates it at 6.1 pawns, against 4.6 for the king move I played.

<figure>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/images/2026-08-20-game2/engine-line-rxf3-exchange-sac.gif' | relative_url }}" alt="Animated engine line: 25.Rxf3 gxf3 26.Rxd7 Rxd7 27.Bxd7">
  <figcaption>25.Rxf3 gxf3 26.Rxd7 Rxd7 27.Bxd7: two bishops and a knight against a rook.</figcaption>
</figure>

That rook sacrifice wasn't giving up material at all. I just needed to see that there was a series of exchanges that would take place on f3 and then again on d7, and what would be left afterwards: two bishops and a knight against a single rook, with the pawns level. With plenty of pawns left on the board there would also be chances for promotion.

The second mistake was not seeing how to recover after allowing the knight fork. The important idea is **Bf4**. On **29.Kh3 Nxf1 30.Bf4** the bishop and the rook on d6 line up on the b8-h2 diagonal against the king on b8, so any rook move becomes a discovered check. Black has to spend a move on **30...Ka8** to defuse it, and then **31.e5** clears the f6 pawn out of the way and the f-pawn runs. Stockfish holds White above five pawns through the whole line.

Instead I played **29.Kf4**, which took the evaluation from 5.1 in my favour to level, and then **30.Bd4** turned level into 4.6 against me. The move there was **30.Bf2**.

### Reflections

**What went well:**

- The opening and the middlegame. Good time management too.
- Spotting the mate threat on move 9 and answering it with a move that guarded g2 and attacked the bishop at the same time.
- Trading queens on the judgement that I had better development and control of the centre, rather than because the material said to.

**What to work on:**

- Recognising that sacrificing the exchange can be the best move, especially when it's part of a sequence.
- Considering the tension across the whole board, and where the position ends up after all of the forcing moves are played.
- Forks. It's a recurring problem for me. I think I've avoided blundering my queen in the last few sessions, but forks are still a blind spot.
